Local SEO for plastic surgeons is the work that puts your practice in front of patients nearby at the moment they search for a procedure.

Those searches look like "rhinoplasty near me," "tummy tuck" plus a city, or "breast augmentation surgeon" plus a neighborhood.

Google answers most of them with a map and three listings, then a page of organic results, and the practices in both places get the calls.

Ranking a plastic surgery practice there is a longer game than ranking a med spa, because the queries are high-value and the competition is real.

But the mechanics are the same two layers, applied to surgery, with one wrinkle med spas never face.

## <span className="he">📍</span> Why local visibility matters more than traffic for plastic surgeons

A surgical patient can't book a procedure with a practice three states away, so national traffic mostly doesn't pay.

What pays is online visibility to the people who can realistically become patients: within driving distance, researching the procedures you perform.

A thousand visitors from the wrong city are worth less than a dozen from your own.

That's why this service is local SEO first, and why we judge it by consult requests rather than raw traffic.

Done well, SEO builds the things ads can't buy: trust from showing up again and again during a long research cycle, a practice name patients recognize, and traffic that keeps coming without a per-click cost.

## <span className="he">🗺️</span> How Google ranks plastic surgeons in Google Maps and the 3-Pack

Google says it ranks local results on three things: relevance, distance, and prominence.

Relevance is how well your listing and website match the search, distance is how far you are from the searcher, and prominence is how well known and well reviewed your practice is.

You can't move your office, so the work goes into relevance and prominence.

## <span className="he">🏥</span> Google Business Profile for a plastic surgery practice

Your Google Business Profile (still called Google My Business, or GMB, by many practices) is the listing that appears in Google Maps and the 3-Pack.

Start with categories: the primary category should be the one that best describes the practice, typically plastic surgeon or cosmetic surgeon, with secondary categories added only where they accurately describe what you do.

Then list the procedures you perform as services, add real photos of the practice, and keep hours and contact details current.

This is where the practitioner-versus-practice question bites.

Multi-surgeon practices have to decide how listings map to individual surgeons versus the practice itself.

Get it wrong and you split your ranking signal across competing listings, so nothing ranks well.

Get it right and the practice and each surgeon reinforce each other.

The details are in our guide to [Google Business Profile for surgeons](/blog/plastic-surgery-google-business-profile/), and practices with more than one office should read [multi-location SEO for plastic surgery](/blog/plastic-surgery-multi-location-seo/), where each location needs its own profile and its own genuinely local landing page.

## <span className="he">📇</span> Citations: the same name, address, and phone everywhere

A citation is any listing of your practice name, address, and phone number on another site, such as a directory or a medical listing platform.

When those details disagree, Google has less reason to trust any of them.

Citation cleanup means finding the listings that are wrong or outdated, including old addresses and former surgeon names, and making them match.

## <span className="he">⭐</span> Reviews and plastic surgeon reputation management

For surgical decisions, reviews move both the ranking and the patient.

Plastic surgeon reputation management mostly comes down to three habits: ask satisfied patients for a review, reply to every review, and keep your practice details consistent on the platforms patients check.

Replies need care: never confirm in a reply that the reviewer is your patient, even when they said so first, because that can be a disclosure of health information.

Industry platforms matter too, and our guide to [reviews and RealSelf for plastic surgeons](/blog/plastic-surgery-reviews-realself/) covers how to handle both.

## <span className="he">🔍</span> Keyword research: plastic surgery SEO starts with search intent

Patients search by procedure, concern, and question, not by your practice name.

Keyword research finds those searches and sorts them by intent: "rhinoplasty cost" and "rhinoplasty surgeon near me" are close to booking, while "what is a deep plane facelift" is earlier research.

That map of searches becomes the topical map, the plan for which pages your website needs and how they link together.

## <span className="he">📝</span> Procedure pages, FAQs, and on-page SEO for plastic surgery

Your patient-facing procedure pages are your topical authority.

Structured well, they tell Google your practice is the answer for "rhinoplasty" or "breast augmentation" in your market.

That means clear, comprehensive coverage of each procedure, internally linked into a topical structure, not a thin page per procedure that says nothing a competitor doesn't.

Each page should answer what patients ask before they book: candidacy, recovery, results, risks, and cost or financing, with a short FAQ for the questions your front desk hears most.

On-page optimization is the structure that makes that content easy for Google to read:

| Element | What it does | Illustrative example (rhinoplasty page) |
|---|---|---|
| Title tag | The clickable headline in search results | Rhinoplasty in [City] with Dr. [Name] |
| H1 and subheadings | Tell Google and patients what each section answers | Recovery, cost, candidacy, before and after |
| Meta description | The summary under the title in search results | What to expect, who it's for, how to book a consult |
| URL | A short, readable address for the procedure | /rhinoplasty/ |
| Internal links | Connect the procedure to its gallery and related procedures | Links to the gallery and to revision rhinoplasty |
| FAQ section | Answers the near-booking questions in the patient's words | "How long is rhinoplasty recovery?" |

It also means the pages that rank are the pages that convert, so procedure-page SEO and [conversion](/plastic-surgery-cro/) are the same work seen from two angles.

The full walkthrough is in [procedure pages that rank and convert](/blog/plastic-surgery-procedure-pages-seo/).

## <span className="he">🖼️</span> Before-and-after galleries as SEO content

A before-and-after gallery is the strongest proof a surgical practice has, and it's also content Google can index.

Organize it by procedure and link each gallery to its procedure page, so the two support each other.

Give every image a descriptive file name, alt text that names the procedure and angle, and a short caption with useful context such as the procedure and time since surgery.

Consent comes first: only publish photos the patient has authorized in writing for that use, and never put a patient's name in a file name, caption, or alt text.

More on layout and the gating debate in [before-and-after galleries](/blog/plastic-surgery-before-after-galleries/).

## <span className="he">📚</span> Educational content, YMYL, and E-E-A-T

Google's search quality rater guidelines treat health topics as "Your Money or Your Life" (YMYL) content, held to a higher standard because bad information can hurt people.

The same guidelines describe E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust.

For a plastic surgery practice, that means educational content with a named surgeon as author or reviewer, their credentials on the page, and claims that stay accurate and conservative.

Blog posts and patient guides are part of the topical-authority content plan, because they answer the earlier research questions and link patients toward procedure pages.

We plan and structure that content, but we don't ghostwrite clinical content your surgeons have to stand behind.

Technical SEO isn't glamorous, but a slow or broken website undercuts everything above it.

The basics: pages that load quickly on phones, a layout that works on a small screen, HTTPS across the site, and healthy Core Web Vitals in Google Search Console.

Patients often research on their phones, so mobile speed is also a conversion issue.

## <span className="he">🔗</span> Backlinks: earned, not bought

Links from other reputable sites are part of what Google counts as prominence.

For a surgical practice, the useful ones are earned: local press, community involvement, professional memberships that list their members, and educational content other sites want to cite.

Buying links breaks Google's spam policies and puts the whole site at risk.

We do citation cleanup, not link-building campaigns, so treat backlinks as a byproduct of being worth citing.

## <span className="he">⏳</span> Why cosmetic surgeon SEO is a longer game than med spa SEO

Plastic surgery SERPs are a different field from med spa SERPs.

| | Med spa searches | Plastic surgery searches |
|---|---|---|
| Who you're up against | Often local businesses with half-finished profiles | Practices that have invested in SEO for years, plus directories like RealSelf |
| How fast rankings move | SEO can move quickly | Profile gains in weeks, competitive procedure rankings over months |
| The listing question | Usually one profile per location | The practice plus individual surgeon listings |
| Content stakes | Treatment information | YMYL surgical content that needs surgeon review |
| What the patient weighs | Price, availability, convenience | The surgeon, results, recovery, and financing |

That doesn't make ranking impossible, it makes patience and consistency the strategy.

Profile and review gains show within weeks, but competitive procedure rankings are a multi-month build, and anyone promising page one in 30 days is selling you something.

## <span className="he">🛡️</span> HIPAA and tracking pixels

Tracking pixels and analytics on a surgical website can send data tying a person to a procedure, or to being a patient, to third-party platforms.

That can be an impermissible disclosure of protected health information under HIPAA.

We set up tracking on what we build with that risk in mind, but we don't claim a certified HIPAA-compliant setup, and your compliance advisor should review yours.

The details are in [HIPAA and website tracking](/blog/hipaa-website-tracking/).

## <span className="he">💸</span> SEO vs. pay-per-click

Pay-per-click (PPC) ads on Google buy a spot above the results for as long as you keep paying.

In our experience, plastic surgery search clicks are very expensive, and Meta ads get aesthetic practices better results for the money.

So we don't run Google Ads or PPC; for paid demand we run [managed Meta ads](/plastic-surgeon-advertising/), and SEO builds the rankings you own.

## <span className="he">📏</span> What to measure: results tied to consults

Rankings are a means, not the goal, so track the metrics tied to consults:

- Map-pack position for your core procedure-and-city queries, checked from a consistent location
- Google Business Profile calls, website clicks, and direction requests
- Organic consult requests by landing page, from the forms and pages we build and track

If rankings climb but consults don't, the traffic is landing on a page that leaks, which is a [conversion problem](/plastic-surgery-cro/), not an SEO one.
