Plastic surgery has some of the most expensive clicks in all of advertising, because a single surgery is worth so much and incumbents bid hard for the same patients.

That fact cuts both ways.

It makes a leaky page the costliest problem in the practice, because you pay premium prices for clicks and then lose them after they land.

And it makes lead generation, done right, one of the highest-leverage decisions a surgical practice can make.

This page is about doing it right: ads you own, sent to a page built to convert, with the testing that keeps it improving.

## <span className="he">🎯</span> What usually goes wrong with surgical ad budgets

The usual surgical marketing arrangement is a retainer, often priced as a percentage of ad spend, plus the ad spend itself.

That structure rewards spending more, not converting better.

The ads get launched, the creative runs until it fatigues, and the traffic goes to whatever page was easiest to link: usually the homepage or a procedure page written for SEO.

Nobody is testing the page, because the page isn't in anyone's scope.

<Callout type="key">

The more expensive your clicks are, the more the landing page matters. A page that converts 4% instead of 2% halves what each consult request costs you, on the same budget.

That is why the landing page is inside the managed-ads scope here, with a new A/B test every month, instead of left as an afterthought.

</Callout>

## <span className="he">🪣</span> Why the ads don't touch your website

When you pay premium prices for high-intent surgical clicks and then send them to your website, you lose them to the same three things every time: weak galleries, buried financing, and slow follow-up.

Your site was built to inform patients who already know you, not to convert a cold, high-consideration prospect into a booked consult.

The page is where the money is won or lost.

## <span className="he">📣</span> What surgical ads have to do differently

Surgical prospects don't book on impulse.

They research for weeks or months, compare surgeons, and look hard at results, credentials, and financing before they ever fill out a form.

So the ads can't just shout an offer.

A month of creative typically rotates through outcomes, surgeon credibility, financing, and a clear consult offer, and the landing page has to answer the same questions in the same order.

Meta's policies also shape the copy: cosmetic procedure ads face restrictions on before-and-after imagery, and ads can't imply they know something about the viewer's body.

So the ads sell the outcome without calling anyone out, and the landing page, which isn't under the same ad rules, carries the galleries and the detail.

That kind of policy nuance is part of the management, not something you have to learn.
