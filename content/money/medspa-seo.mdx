## <span className="he">🗺️</span> Google Maps and your Google Business Profile

Google says it ranks local results on three things: relevance, distance, and prominence.

You can't move your front door, so the work goes into relevance and prominence, and your Google Business Profile is where most of it starts.

<LocalPack query="botox near me" you="Your med spa" category="Med spa" caption="Illustrative, not a client result." />

Optimizing the profile means:

- **The right primary category:** the most specific one that accurately describes your business, which for most practices is Medical spa
- **Every treatment listed as a service,** so Google can match you to each treatment search
- **Real photos** of your space and team, plus accurate hours and a working booking link
- **One profile per location,** each with its own [location page](/blog/medspa-multi-location-seo/) if you run more than one

Ranking for "near me" searches works the same way: you don't need "near me" in your copy, because Google uses the searcher's location and then picks the most relevant, most prominent practices nearby.

The full walkthrough is in our [Google Business Profile guide](/blog/medspa-google-business-profile/) and [how to rank a med spa on Google Maps](/blog/how-to-rank-medspa-google-maps/).

## <span className="he">⭐</span> Reviews: ask, reply, and keep it HIPAA-safe

Google says review count and review score factor into local ranking.

Reviews are also what convinces the person reading the map pack to click you over the practice next door.

A steady habit beats a one-time push: ask happy patients at checkout, make the link easy to find, and reply to every review.

<Callout type="key">

Keep every reply HIPAA-safe: never confirm that someone is a patient or mention their treatment, even if they did first.

</Callout>

Reviews come from your patients and your front desk, so this is advice rather than a service we run, and [getting more reviews](/blog/medspa-get-more-reviews/) covers the system, while the [FTC rules on reviews](/blog/ftc-testimonial-rules-medspa/) cover what you can't do.

## <span className="he">🎯</span> Keyword research: treatments plus your city

Patients rarely search for "med spa."

They search for the treatment they want, like "lip filler," "hydrafacial near me," or "laser hair removal" plus their city.

Each of those is its own ranking battle, and you can rank for one while staying invisible for another.

Good keyword research finds those treatment-and-city searches, plus the long-tail keywords that come earlier in the decision, like "botox vs dysport" or "how many sessions for laser hair removal."

That list becomes the topical map: the plan for which pages your website needs and how they link together.

The method is in [med spa keyword research](/blog/medspa-keyword-research/).

## <span className="he">📝</span> Treatment pages: one per treatment

One "services" page listing twenty treatments can't be the best answer to twenty different searches.

A separate page per treatment can, because it answers that treatment's questions in depth.

<Card icon="check" title="Proof and questions">

</Card>

On-page SEO is the structure that makes that content easy for Google to read:

| Element | What it does | Illustrative example (Botox page) |
|---|---|---|
| Title tag | The clickable headline in search results | Botox in [City] with [Provider], RN |
| Meta description | The summary under the title | What to expect, who it's for, how to book |
| H1 and subheadings | Tell Google and patients what each section answers | Cost, results, downtime, FAQs |
| Internal links | Connect related treatments and the gallery | Links to filler and the Botox gallery |

Internal links between treatment pages are how the topical structure takes shape: each page supports the others, and Google sees the practice covers the whole subject.

The full list of basics is in the [med spa SEO checklist](/blog/medspa-seo-checklist/).

## <span className="he">📚</span> Content marketing for long-tail searches

Blog posts and patient guides answer the earlier research questions, then link patients toward the treatment page.

That's content marketing done for SEO: every article fills a gap in the topical map, rather than a weekly post on whatever came to mind.

Health topics are held to a higher standard in Google's quality rater guidelines, which describe this as E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust).

So name the provider who performs or reviews each treatment, show their credentials, and keep claims accurate.

We plan and structure the content, but we don't ghostwrite clinical claims you can't stand behind.

Before-and-after galleries count as content too, and [before-and-after photo rules](/blog/medspa-before-after-photos/) covers consent, which comes first.

## <span className="he">⚡</span> Technical SEO: speed, mobile, and schema

A slow or broken website undercuts everything above it.

Patients often search on their phones, so pages need to load fast and work on a small screen.

Check Core Web Vitals in Google Search Console, and make sure no page you want ranked is accidentally blocked from Google.

Add LocalBusiness schema, or a more specific type such as MedicalBusiness, with the same name, address, and phone number as your profile.

Our guide to [website speed and mobile UX](/blog/medspa-website-speed-mobile/) covers what to check.

The audit flags what's holding your pages back, but full website rebuilds aren't part of the retainer, because we don't build full websites.

## <span className="he">📇</span> Citations and backlinks

A citation is any listing of your practice name, address, and phone number (NAP) on another site, such as a directory.

When those details disagree, Google has less reason to trust any of them, so citation cleanup means making every listing match.

Links from reputable local sites also count toward prominence, but they should be earned, through local press, community involvement, and content worth citing.

We do citation cleanup, not link-building campaigns, and the details are in [citations and directories](/blog/medspa-citations-directories/).

## <span className="he">⚖️</span> Competing with chains, dermatologists, and plastic surgeons

Most markets have national med spa chains, dermatology groups, and plastic surgery practices chasing the same treatment searches.

Chains bring brand recognition and budget, and physician practices bring credentials.

An independent medical spa still has real advantages in the map pack: no one is closer to your neighborhood, and pages written for your city give Google a better match than a template.

No one can promise you'll outrank them, but it's a fight independent practices can win.

## <span className="he">🤖</span> AI search, as a side effect

Patients increasingly ask ChatGPT and Google's AI Overviews where to get a treatment.

Those tools lean on sites that cover a subject thoroughly, which is exactly what topical-authority SEO builds.

That visibility is a bonus of doing SEO properly, not a separate product we sell.

## <span className="he">🛡️</span> HIPAA and tracking

Tracking pixels and analytics on a med spa website can send data tying a person to a treatment to third-party platforms.

That can be an impermissible disclosure of protected health information.

We set up tracking on what we build with that risk in mind, but your compliance advisor should review your setup, and [HIPAA and website tracking](/blog/hipaa-website-tracking/) explains the risk.
