The average medspa revenue figure most owners quote comes from one place: the American Med Spa Association (AmSpa).

Its 2024 Medical Spa State of the Industry Report puts average annual medical spa revenue at **$1,398,833**.

That single number is useful, but it hides a wide spread between practices, and it says nothing about how much of that revenue an owner keeps.

This page covers the benchmark figures AmSpa publishes, what drives med spa profit margins, what owners take home, how med spas are valued, and the levers that move a practice above the average.

## <span className="he">📊</span> The AmSpa benchmarks

AmSpa is the trade association for the medical spa industry, and its State of the Industry report is built on a survey of med spa owners and professionals.

The figures below come from [AmSpa's recap of its 2024 Medical Spa State of the Industry Report](https://www.americanmedspa.org/news/2024-medical-spa-state-of-the-industry-executive-report-recap/), the latest edition with a public summary.

| Metric | 2024 report | Previous report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average annual med spa revenue | $1,398,833 | $1,307,587 |
| Average spend per visit | $527 | $536 |
| Patient visits per month | 245 | 245 in 2018 |
| Repeat patients | 73% | 65% |
| U.S. medical spas | 10,488 | 8,899 |
| Newly opened in the survey year | 18% | 17% |
| Single-location practices | 81% | not reported |
| Female patients | 89% | not reported |

That shift in repeat patients, from 65% to 73%, is the most useful number in the report for an owner trying to grow.

Visits are driven by new patients and, more heavily, by returning ones.

Spend per visit is driven by service mix, pricing, packages, add-ons, and retail.

That's why two practices with the same number of rooms can land far apart: one fills its calendar with repeat patients buying series, the other keeps paying to replace one-time visitors.

### Patient visits and spend per visit

AmSpa's average practice sees 245 patient visits a month, the same count it reported in 2018.

Patients spend an average of $527 per visit, down slightly from $536.

If the average visit count has barely moved in years, growth for an individual practice has to come from fuller schedules and higher value per patient, not only from more new patients.

### Who the patients are

AmSpa reports that 89% of medical spa patients are female.

Among female patients, 54% are 35 to 54 years old, 24% are 55 or older, and 22% are 18 to 34.

That profile shapes which services sell, which messages land, and when patients book.

## <span className="he">💉</span> Service mix and what each line earns

Revenue per visit and margin both depend heavily on which treatments a med spa sells.

AmSpa's public recap doesn't break revenue down by treatment, so the table below describes how each service line typically earns rather than quoting shares.

| Service | How it earns | What squeezes margin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neuromodulators (Botox and similar) | Patients return every few months, which makes this the anchor for repeat visits | Product cost per unit and price competition |
| Dermal fillers | Higher ticket per visit | Higher product cost per syringe |
| Laser hair removal | Sold as a package series | Device cost, so room and device utilization matter |
| Body contouring (cryolipolysis) | High ticket per treatment plan | Device cost plus per-cycle consumables |
| Microneedling and RF microneedling | Sold as a series, pairs with skincare | Single-use tips and device cost |
| Chemical peels | Low product cost, easy add-on or series | Low ticket unless packaged |
| IV therapy | Frequent, repeatable visits | Supplies and staff time per drip |
| Medical weight loss (GLP-1) | Monthly program revenue | Medication cost and provider oversight |
| Retail skincare | Raises spend per visit with no treatment time | Inventory and product cost |

Treatment-level marketing is covered in our guides to [Botox](/blog/botox-marketing/), [dermal fillers](/blog/dermal-filler-marketing/), [laser hair removal](/blog/laser-hair-removal-marketing/), [CoolSculpting and body contouring](/blog/coolsculpting-marketing/), [Morpheus8 and microneedling](/blog/morpheus8-marketing/), [chemical peels](/blog/chemical-peel-marketing/), [IV therapy](/blog/iv-therapy-marketing/), and [medical weight loss](/blog/medical-weight-loss-marketing/).

Packages, bundles, and retail are the simplest ways to raise spend per visit.

A laser hair removal package or a peel series books several visits at once, and a combination treatment plan raises the ticket without adding a new patient.

Retail skincare adds revenue at checkout without using a treatment room or provider hour.

Gross margin on most aesthetic treatments is healthy, because the product in a treatment costs far less than the price of the treatment.

Net margin is where practices separate, because labor, rent, and equipment are mostly fixed while revenue swings month to month.

Labor is the line owners feel most.

Provider pay is often tied to production, so an empty hour on a salaried provider's schedule costs margin directly.

Staff turnover adds hidden cost too, in recruiting, training, and the patients who follow a departing injector.

Hiring is its own challenge in this industry, which is why Gabe also runs [AestheticHires](https://www.aesthetichires.com/), a job board for med spa roles.

The medical director is a real cost line as well.

AmSpa reports that 48% of medical spas hired a medical director in the past year, so budget for the role and its fee.

Marketing is the cost line with the widest range, and our [med spa marketing cost](/medspa-marketing-cost/) page breaks down what different approaches cost.

[Local SEO](/medspa-seo/) is the channel that keeps working when you stop paying for clicks, since rankings and reviews compound over time.

Compliance costs are smaller but real: licensing, charting, and keeping patient data out of ad-tracking tools.

Tracking pixels on a med spa website can leak patient information, which is a HIPAA risk; our guide to [HIPAA and website tracking](/blog/hipaa-website-tracking/) explains how.

Many states also restrict who can own a medical practice under the corporate practice of medicine (CPOM) doctrine, and the rules vary by state, so ownership structure is a question for a healthcare attorney.

## <span className="he">✅</span> Are med spas profitable?

Many are, and the industry data points in a healthy direction.

AmSpa reports average revenue rising, repeat patients climbing to 73%, and 84% of owners expecting revenue to increase, with more than half of those expecting growth of at least 10%.

But the same report shows 18% of med spas were newly opened in its survey year, so competition for the same local patients keeps rising.

A med spa is profitable when full schedules and repeat patients cover fixed costs with room to spare.

It struggles when it pays to acquire patients who never return, or when providers sit idle between bookings.

If you're asking whether opening a med spa is worth it, start with the investment and ramp in [what it costs to open a med spa](/blog/cost-to-open-a-medspa/).

New practices spend months building a patient base before revenue approaches the averages above.

## <span className="he">🏢</span> Ownership structure and revenue differences

Ownership explains part of why revenue varies so much between medical spas.

AmSpa reports that 81% of med spas are single-location, while multi-location groups average nine locations.

Multi-location practices average 12 employees at their primary location, versus eight at a single-location practice.

Among single-owner practices, 67% are owned by someone other than a physician or surgeon, and nurse practitioners have nearly pulled even with MDs as the largest owner group.

Only 3% of medical spas are owned by private equity firms, according to AmSpa.

That means the "average" med spa in the data is mostly an independent, single-location business, which is the right comparison for most owners reading this.

## <span className="he">💼</span> Owner pay, salary, and valuation

Owner take-home is the question behind many searches for average med spa revenue, and it's the one with the least reliable public data.

AmSpa's public recap doesn't publish owner compensation, so we don't quote a salary figure.

What an owner takes home depends on three things: the practice's profit, whether the owner is also a treating provider, and how the profit is taken.

An owner who also treats patients can pay themselves for that clinical work like any provider, with profit on top.

Profit can then be taken as salary, as distributions (dividends in some structures), or left as retained earnings to fund equipment, a second location, or a cash cushion.

The right mix depends on your entity and tax situation, which is a conversation for your accountant.

### Medspa valuation

Published valuation multiples for med spas vary widely and rarely come from a primary source, so we don't quote one.

Qualitatively, buyers generally start from earnings after adjusting owner pay to a market rate, then judge how dependable those earnings are.

<Checklist>

- A high share of repeat patients and membership revenue
- Revenue spread across several providers, not dependent on one injector
- Clean books that separate product, labor, and overhead
- A patient base that comes from channels the practice owns, like search rankings and a patient list
- More than one location, or a proven model that could support one

</Checklist>

The same things that raise a practice's value also raise its margin today.

## <span className="he">📈</span> Market size and industry trends

AmSpa sizes the U.S. medical aesthetics industry at more than $17 billion, growing by more than $1 billion a year.

The number of medical spas rose from 8,899 to 10,488 between the report's last two survey years.

Our [med spa industry statistics](/blog/medspa-industry-statistics/) page covers market size and growth in more depth.

AmSpa's recap names IV therapy and medical wellness among the industry's newer service lines.

Medical weight loss with GLP-1 medications has become another monthly program many med spas add, with its own compliance and cost profile.

Combination treatment plans, where one visit pairs a neuromodulator with a skin treatment, raise spend per visit without adding patients.

### Revenue per hour and provider utilization

Revenue per hour (RPh) is revenue divided by the provider hours you had available.

Provider utilization is booked treatment hours divided by available hours.

Together they show whether growth needs more patients or just fuller schedules, which is the cheaper fix.

Online booking, automated reminders, and a clean schedule template all push utilization up, and our [med spa booking software](/blog/medspa-booking-software/) guide compares the options.

Track both alongside the other [med spa KPIs](/blog/medspa-kpis/) that tie directly to revenue.

### Client retention and memberships

With nearly three in four patients being repeat patients, retention is the biggest revenue line most med spas have.

Rebooking at checkout, treatment plans that span several visits, and follow-up after each appointment keep those patients on the calendar.

A [membership program](/blog/medspa-membership-programs/) turns that loyalty into predictable monthly revenue.

### Conversion of the demand you already have

Before paying for more traffic, check how much of your current traffic becomes booked consults.

A faster, clearer path from your website to a booking lifts revenue without raising acquisition cost, and it makes every marketing dollar work harder.
