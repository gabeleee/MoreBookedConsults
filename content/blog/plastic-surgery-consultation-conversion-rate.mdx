- **It's a chain of rates,** not one: visitor, inquiry, consult, surgery.
- **Median consult to surgery:** 30 to 50 percent; 60 percent or more is strong.
- **Say which window you measure:** 30 days, 90 days, or 6 months after the consult.
- **Cheapest stage to fix:** visitor to inquiry, on the website, no extra spend.
- **A 30% inquiry-to-consult rate** is a follow-up problem, not a talent problem.

Short answer: the median plastic surgery consultation to surgery conversion rate is **30 to 50 percent,** and 60 percent or more is strong.

Upstream, a healthy practice turns **2 to 4 percent of website visitors into an inquiry** and **40 to 60 percent of inquiries into a booked consultation.**

Multiply those and the average site turns a low single-digit fraction of visitors into cases, which is exactly why small improvements in the middle of the funnel are worth so much.

This guide is for the plastic surgeon, the practice manager, and the patient care coordinator who share ownership of that number.

## <span className="he">📊</span> Plastic surgery conversion rates at each stage

A plastic surgery practice doesn't have one conversion rate; it has a chain of them.

Each stage is a separate benchmark and a separate fix.

| Stage | Poor | Median | Good |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visitor → inquiry | under 1.5% | 2 to 4% | 6%+ |
| Inquiry → booked consult | under 30% | 40 to 60% | 70%+ |
| Consult → surgery | under 25% | 30 to 50% | 60%+ |
| Visitor → surgery (blended) | under 0.3% | 0.5 to 1% | 1.5%+ |

Judge each stage on its own row.

A practice with great surgeons and a 30% inquiry-to-consult rate doesn't have a talent problem.

It has a follow-up problem.

## <span className="he">🎯</span> Why website conversion is the cheapest stage to fix

The visitor-to-inquiry stage is the only one you can improve **without spending a dollar more on ads and without adding staff.**

It's just the site: [inquiry forms](/blog/plastic-surgery-website-design/), procedure pages, [before/after galleries](/blog/plastic-surgery-before-after-galleries/), and [financing clarity](/blog/plastic-surgery-financing-marketing/).

That's why our [plastic surgery CRO](/plastic-surgery-cro/) work starts here: the leverage is highest and the cost is lowest.

The consult-to-surgery stage belongs to your surgeon and your coordinator, and the rest of this guide covers it because the numbers only make sense as a chain.

## <span className="he">🔧</span> What moves each stage

Each stage has its own set of levers, and confusing them is how practices spend on the wrong fix.

<Steps>

<Step title="Visitor to inquiry">

Shorter forms, clearer procedure pricing signals, and ungated [before/after galleries](/blog/plastic-surgery-before-after-galleries/). This is pure website work, no ad spend required.

</Step>

<Step title="Inquiry to consult">

This stage is won or lost on speed and ownership: [speed-to-lead and follow-up](/blog/plastic-surgery-lead-management/), and a [patient coordinator](/blog/patient-coordinator-consult-conversion/) who owns the booking. An inquiry that waits a day for a reply is often already gone.

</Step>

<Step title="Consult to surgery">

Here the levers are a clear fee quote, [financing presented well](/blog/plastic-surgery-financing-marketing/), [virtual consult options](/blog/virtual-plastic-surgery-consultations/) for out-of-market patients, and the in-consult experience itself.

</Step>

</Steps>

<Callout type="key">

The middle stage, inquiry to consult, is where most surgical practices bleed the most, and it's almost always a follow-up speed problem, not a marketing one.

Fixing it usually costs nothing but a faster process.

</Callout>

## <span className="he">📏</span> How to measure your consultation conversion rate

Before you compare yourself with any benchmark, define the number the same way every month.

<Equation title="Consult-to-surgery rate" op="÷" result="Conversion rate" caption="Count consultations actually held. No-shows are a separate metric, not a failed close.">
<Term label="Surgeries booked" note="date set and deposit paid" />
<Term label="Consultations held" note="same cohort of patients" />
</Equation>

A booked surgery means a date on the calendar and a deposit taken, not a patient who said "probably."

A consultation means one that actually happened, so cancellations and no-shows don't drag the close rate down and hide a scheduling problem.

Then pick a time window, because a surgical decision rarely happens in the room.

- **30-day rate:** patients who booked within 30 days of their consult. This is the close at, or right after, the consultation.
- **90-day rate:** adds patients who needed time for financing, scheduling, or a partner conversation.
- **6-month rate:** captures the long-tail deciders and gives the truest picture of the consult itself.

Report which window your number uses.

Comparing your 30-day rate with another practice's 6-month rate is how practices talk themselves into, or out of, a problem that isn't real.

Surgical decision cycles run 3 to 12 months, so last-click attribution lies.

A consult that closes into surgery in March may trace back to a website visit in December, and a naive report will credit whatever the patient clicked last.

Track cohorts by inquiry month, not by close month, and give each cohort a full window to mature before you judge it.

<Callout type="note">

Call tracking matters more here than for a med spa, because a large share of high-intent surgical inquiries come by phone.

An untracked call is an invisible conversion, and it makes your best channel look like your worst.

</Callout>

## <span className="he">🔎</span> Track it by procedure, surgeon and lead source

A single practice-wide rate hides the leak, so split it before you try to fix it.

| Split by | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Procedure | Whether one procedure, say [breast augmentation](/blog/breast-augmentation-marketing/), closes very differently from your facelift or rhinoplasty consults |
| Surgeon | Whether one aesthetic surgeon's consults convert differently from a colleague's, which points to consult style or scheduling |
| Lead source | Whether patients from Google, Meta ads, referrals, or a virtual consult arrive ready to book or still shopping |
| Coordinator | Whether follow-up and the fee conversation are consistent across the team |

Small practices won't have enough consults to split every way every month, so review the splits quarterly.

## <span className="he">🤝</span> The patient care coordinator: an essential role

In many surgical practices, the biggest single lever on consult-to-surgery conversion is a dedicated patient care coordinator.

That person owns every consultation from the booking to a surgery date, so nothing falls between the front desk, the surgeon, and billing.

A good coordinator prepares the patient before the consult, walks through the fee quote after it, and owns the follow-up.

They also build meaningful connections with patients, which matters when someone is weighing a large, personal, expensive decision.

We cover what great coordinators do, and how to invest in the role, in [patient coordinators and consult conversion](/blog/patient-coordinator-consult-conversion/).

## <span className="he">🩺</span> Raising surgical conversion with photos, imaging and simulation

Patients book when they can picture a realistic result from a surgeon they trust.

Show [before-and-after photos](/blog/plastic-surgery-before-after-galleries/) of patients with similar anatomy for the exact procedure being discussed, not a general highlight reel.

Some practices also use digital imaging, 3D simulation, or augmented reality (AR) tools during the consult to help patients understand proportions and options.

Treat any simulation as a conversation aid, and say plainly that it's an approximation, not a promise of a result.

Expectations set honestly in the consult protect the close and the post-op relationship.

## <span className="he">💻</span> Virtual pre-consultations

A virtual consultation (a telehealth pre-consult) lets distant, busy, or hesitant patients take a low-pressure first step.

Patients who start virtually often arrive at the in-person consult already informed about the procedure, the fee range, and the recovery.

We don't know of a reliable published figure for how much virtual consults change the close rate, so measure it yourself by tagging virtual-first patients as their own lead source.

Setup, delivery, and the compliance basics are in our guide to [virtual plastic surgery consultations](/blog/virtual-plastic-surgery-consultations/).

## <span className="he">💵</span> Fee quotes, financing and financial counseling

Cost is where many consults stall, so the fee conversation deserves a process, not an improvised moment at checkout.

- **Give the fee quote in writing before the patient leaves,** with surgeon, anesthesia, and facility fees in one total so nothing surprises them later.
- **Present patient financing and payment plans as the normal way to pay,** with the monthly figure next to the total, not as a brochure on the way out.
- **Offer a short financial counseling conversation** with the coordinator: the deposit, the payment schedule, and what happens next.

A written quote is essential, because patients need something to reread at home and show a partner.

How to place and present financing on your site and in the consult is covered in [plastic surgery financing as a conversion lever](/blog/plastic-surgery-financing-marketing/).

## <span className="he">🛡️</span> Handling patient objections

Four objections come up again and again in surgical consults, and the best coordinators name them before the patient does.

<Cards>

<Card icon="chart" title="Cost">

Turn a large total into a monthly plan with financing, and confirm the quote covers everything.

</Card>

<Card icon="calendar" title="Recovery">

Make it concrete: time off work, help at home, and when normal life resumes, planned around the patient's calendar.

</Card>

<Card icon="shield" title="Complications">

The surgeon answers risk questions honestly and specifically. Brushing past them costs trust.

</Card>

<Card icon="check" title="Partner approval">

Invite the partner to the consult or a follow-up call, so the decision isn't made from a secondhand summary.

</Card>

</Cards>

Write down which objection each unconverted patient raised, because that record is what makes the review below possible.

## <span className="he">🔁</span> Follow-up after the consultation

The decision cycle is long, so the follow-up has to be planned, owned by a person, and useful to the patient.

<Steps>

<Step title="Same day">

A personal thank-you from the coordinator with the written quote and a direct way to reach them.

</Step>

<Step title="Within the first week">

A call to answer the questions that came up at home, which is often when partner and cost concerns surface.

</Step>

<Step title="Across the decision window">

Scheduled check-ins, each with something useful: an answer to a common question, recovery planning, or relevant before-and-after photos.

</Step>

<Step title="When they go quiet">

One clear, polite last check, then a move to a long-term list rather than silence.

</Step>

</Steps>

The tools matter less than the ownership: one named coordinator, a written cadence, and a note of every touch.

For the inquiry-stage side of this, see [plastic surgery lead management](/blog/plastic-surgery-lead-management/).

## <span className="he">📋</span> Reviewing consultations that didn't convert

Improving the rate starts with knowing why patients said no.

Send a short post-consultation survey to patients who didn't book, asking what held them back: cost, timing, recovery, choosing another surgeon, or still deciding.

Then hold a monthly review where the surgeon and coordinator walk through every unconverted consult.

<Checklist>

- Was a written fee quote given before the patient left?
- Were financing and payment plans discussed?
- Which objection did the patient raise, and was it answered?
- Did the follow-up cadence actually happen?
- Did the patient see results relevant to their procedure and anatomy?

</Checklist>

Patterns show up fast, and they point to process fixes rather than more marketing spend.
