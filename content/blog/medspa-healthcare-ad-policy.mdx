Meta's health and wellness ad restrictions hit a med spa in two places at once, and the only workarounds that hold up are the compliant ones.

The first is the ad itself: what your creative and copy are allowed to say and show.

The second is your data: what the Meta Pixel and Conversions API are allowed to send back, and therefore what Meta can optimize toward.

Most guides blur the two, which is why owners fix the wrong one.

This guide covers both, with every rule linked to Meta's own documentation, so you can check your account and decide what to change.

## <span className="he">🔒</span> Why Meta restricts health data

Meta's [Business Tools Terms](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/361948878201809) prohibit advertisers from sending health information through the pixel and related tools.

Meta's own list of prohibited health information includes medical procedures, treatments and testing, prescription medications, and body specifications.

That covers most of what a med spa's website is about.

The pressure behind it is legal as well as reputational.

In February 2023 the FTC took its [first-ever action under the Health Breach Notification Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising) against GoodRx, which it said shared users' prescription medications and health conditions with advertising platforms including Facebook, and GoodRx agreed to a $1.5 million civil penalty.

A month later the FTC [proposed an order against BetterHelp](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook) over sharing consumers' mental health data with Facebook and others for advertising.

In July 2023 the FTC and HHS [warned about 130 hospital systems and telehealth providers](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/07/ftc-hhs-warn-hospital-systems-telehealth-providers-about-privacy-security-risks-online-tracking) that tracking technologies like the Meta Pixel can disclose health conditions, diagnoses, medications and treatments.

The FTC's point in that letter applies directly to aesthetic practices: businesses not covered by HIPAA still have to protect personal health information from unauthorized disclosure.

So Meta filters the data, and the restrictions you see in Events Manager are the result.

### PII vs PHI in plain terms

**PII** (personally identifiable information) is anything that identifies a person: a name, an email address, a phone number.

**PHI** (protected health information) is the HIPAA term for individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity, such as a healthcare provider, or its business associate.

The risk sits where the two meet.

An email address on its own is PII.

The same email address sent to an ad platform alongside "booked a filler consult" connects a person to a treatment, and that is the combination regulators and Meta care about.

The [HIPAA and website tracking guide](/blog/hipaa-website-tracking/) covers where that leak usually hides and how to measure without it.

## <span className="he">🏷️</span> Which businesses Meta classifies as health and wellness

Meta assigns [data source categories](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1402913027039332) to websites and apps based on their topics and the products or services they provide.

Its health and wellness category covers sources associated with medical conditions or health statuses, provider and patient relationships, services for accessing personal health information, or health-related products and services.

Meta's own examples include a patient portal, a telemedicine platform and a pharmacy.

A med spa that offers injectables, lasers and other medical treatments sits squarely inside that description.

The definition is broad enough that adjacent businesses, such as skincare brands, wellness studios and fitness services with a health angle, can land in it too.

There are two ways a category gets assigned.

You can categorize your own dataset, and you can change a category you chose.

Meta can also assign a category itself, and you can't edit a Meta-assigned category, only ask Meta to review it.

## <span className="he">📊</span> Meta's three data restriction levels

When a website domain is in a restricted category, Meta says it [may apply one or more of three restrictions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/511197658391698), which can apply in specific countries or regions or globally.

| Restriction | What Meta restricts | What Meta suggests you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Core setup** | Custom parameters and anything in a URL after the domain | Most campaigns keep running; review custom audiences and events that depend on URLs or custom parameters |
| **Restriction on certain standard events** | Specific mid and lower funnel events | Find alternative events, such as more upper-funnel events, for targeting and optimization |
| **Full restrictions** | All events, in specific regions or all regions | Optimize for objectives that don't rely on your data, such as Awareness, Engagement and Traffic |

Under any of these, custom events are blocked automatically until you review and confirm them in Events Manager.

[Core setup](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/124742407297678) deserves a closer look, because it is the level many advertisers meet first.

A URL like yourpractice.com/botox/thank-you?service=lip-filler reaches Meta as yourpractice.com, and any custom parameter you attached is dropped.

Meta also notes that automatic advanced matching may be unavailable, and that URL-based custom audiences may take longer to fill or stop updating.

You can switch core setup on yourself as a precaution, but if Meta turned it on, you can't turn it off.

## <span className="he">🔍</span> How to check your classification in Events Manager

This tells you whether your campaigns are optimizing the way you set them up.

<Steps>

<Step title="Open your dataset">

In Meta Events Manager, click Datasets in the left menu and select your pixel's dataset.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the category">

Open the Settings tab and scroll to Manage data source categories. If the section is missing, your data source isn't in a category that carries restrictions.

</Step>

<Step title="Check data restrictions">

On the same tab, find Data restrictions and the Core setup section to see whether core setup is on and who turned it on.

</Step>

<Step title="Read the diagnostics">

Open the Diagnostics tab for notices such as Event parameters blocked, and review what was removed and from which pages.

</Step>

<Step title="Review custom events">

Under Data controls, the Manage event blocking section shows custom events waiting for review or blocked by Meta.

</Step>

</Steps>

You need full control of the business portfolio, or an admin role on the ad account that owns the dataset, to see and change these settings.

Meta also says it will tell you by email and in Events Manager when it limits or restricts a data source, so check the inbox tied to your business portfolio.

When Meta detects prohibited information, it removes it and flags it in the Diagnostics tab.

Meta says that if you get those notices repeatedly, it may [put your dataset into core setup for at least 90 days](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/3720004518245751).

## <span className="he">📉</span> What restrictions do to campaign performance

Meta's delivery system learns from the conversions you report.

When lower-funnel events such as a lead are restricted, you can still run campaigns, but the system is optimizing toward a signal further from what you actually want.

That is blind optimization: Meta finds people who click or land, not necessarily people who book.

That doesn't make Meta ads useless for a med spa.

It means the proof of what works has to come from your own booking data, and the landing page has to convert more of the traffic Meta sends.

## <span className="he">📡</span> Conversions API and server-side tracking: what they fix and what they don't

The Conversions API sends events from your server to Meta instead of relying only on the browser pixel.

Many guides present it as the workaround for health and wellness restrictions.

It isn't one.

Meta lists the [Conversions API as one of its Business Tools](https://www.facebook.com/help/331509497253087), alongside the Meta Pixel, and its category restrictions limit what event data you can share through those tools.

If your data source is restricted, server events are restricted too.

- Control over exactly which fields you send
- Cleaner data for the events you're still allowed to share

</Side>

<Side title="What it won't do" tone="no">

For an account without restrictions, the Meta Pixel plus the Conversions API is the setup that lets Meta optimize toward real leads.

For a restricted account, the Conversions API still helps with measurement, but it may not restore lower-funnel optimization.

Any vendor that promises otherwise is promising something Meta's own documentation doesn't support.

## <span className="he">⚠️</span> Renaming events and parameters: the workaround to avoid

The most common "hack" is to rename a restricted standard event, such as sending a Lead as a custom event with a vague name, so Meta can optimize on it again.

Meta's terms address this directly.

The names you choose for events, conversions and custom audiences [must not reflect, imply or be based on prohibited information](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/361948878201809), and Meta says you [must not attempt to send data it has previously detected and removed](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/414614186228298).

New custom events are blocked until you review them, and Meta may block custom events it detects on its own.

If Meta declines your review request for a blocked custom event, you can't request another one.

The consequences Meta lists run from performance issues and data restrictions to suspension of your use of its business tools.

For a practice whose new patients come from Meta, that risk outweighs any short-term gain.

What is legitimate is data minimization: sending Meta less health detail in the first place.

<Checklist>

- Keep treatment and condition names out of thank-you page URLs and query strings
- Don't pass form answers, such as the treatment a patient picked, into pixel parameters
- Send only the standard parameters you actually use
- Name custom events for the action, such as a form submit, never for a treatment
- Turn on core setup yourself if you can't audit every page quickly

</Checklist>

Do this because it is the right way to handle patient data, not to change your category.

A clean setup reduces what can leak and what Meta has to strip out, but it won't undo a category Meta assigned based on what your practice offers.

## <span className="he">🎯</span> Custom and lookalike audience restrictions

Meta [flags custom audiences](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1055828013359808) that include information its terms don't allow, such as references to specific health conditions.

A flagged audience can't be used in new campaigns, and Meta advises editing or pausing active campaigns that use one.

Lookalike audiences inherit the problem: if the seed audience is flagged, so is the lookalike built on it.

Custom conversions follow the same logic.

Meta's developer documentation says that from September 2, 2025 it began rolling out [proactive restrictions on custom conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/meta-pixel/implementation/conversion-tracking/) that suggest specific health conditions.

Naming matters here as much as content.

An audience called "Botox patients" or built on the rule "URL contains /tummy-tuck" is the kind of thing that gets flagged.

Safer bases are engagement audiences, broad location and age targeting, and customer lists that carry no treatment detail.

If you believe an audience was flagged in error, you can request a review from Ads Manager or Audience Manager.

## <span className="he">🖼️</span> Meta ads creative rules: before-and-afters, personal attributes and Botox

The creative side is governed by Meta's [Health and Wellness ad standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/), which name dermal fillers, injectable treatments, chemical peels, micro-needling and laser treatments among the cosmetic procedures advertisers can promote.

Ads for cosmetic products, procedures or surgeries must target people 18 and older.

Under the current version, those ads can depict a before-and-after transformation.

They can't contain statements of inferiority about physical appearance, meaning copy that is negative and attacks a person's appearance, specific body parts or hygiene.

They also can't use clickbait, such as exaggerated claims or promises of specific outcomes within a set timeframe without disclaimers or qualifiers.

Meta has revised this standard more than once, so check the change log on the policy page before you build a campaign around results photos.

Before-and-after photos also need specific written patient consent, which the [before-and-after photo guide](/blog/medspa-before-after-photos/) covers.

For one treatment worked through end to end, the [laser hair removal marketing](/blog/laser-hair-removal-marketing/) playbook shows how these rules shape the ads and the "permanent hair reduction" wording.

A second policy catches more med spa copy than the first.

Meta's [personal attributes policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/) bars ads that assert or imply someone's physical or mental health, including medical conditions.

Brand-name injectables add a third layer.

Botox and other neuromodulators are prescription drugs, which fall under Meta's [Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/) rather than the health and wellness standard alone.

The practical route is to sell the outcome and the offer in the ad and let the landing page explain the treatment, as the [Botox marketing guide](/blog/botox-marketing/) and the [Botox lead generation page](/botox-lead-generation/) describe.

## <span className="he">🔎</span> Google's healthcare ad policies, briefly

Google runs separate rules, and the short version is similar in spirit.

Google's [personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465) treats health as a sensitive interest category.

Health-related ads can't use advertiser-curated audiences, which rules out remarketing lists and customer match for them.

Prescription drug terms are restricted on Google as well.

We don't manage Google Ads, but if you run them yourself, the [med spa Google Ads guide](/blog/medspa-google-ads/) covers campaigns that stay inside those rules.

## <span className="he">📨</span> Appealing a misclassification

If Meta assigned your website to health and wellness and you believe that's wrong, you can [ask for a review](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/467621355878794).

In Events Manager, open Manage data source categories, click View details, then Request review.

Meta says it will email you its decision, but it doesn't publish how long reviews take.

Be realistic about the outcome.

A practice that performs injectables and laser treatments fits Meta's own definition, so a review is most useful when the category is plainly wrong, not when it's inconvenient.

Blocked custom events, flagged audiences and flagged custom conversions each have their own review button, in Events Manager, Audience Manager or Ads Manager.

## <span className="he">🧾</span> Running compliant campaigns

Getting approved and staying measurable is mostly about respecting the category from the start.

<Checklist>

- Check your data source category and core setup status in Events Manager
- Keep targeting general, 18 and older, and free of health attributes
- Write copy that never attacks appearance or implies a condition
- Keep brand-name prescription drugs out of the ad unless you're authorized
- Send clicks to a clear, honest [landing page](/blog/medspa-landing-pages/) that matches the ad
- Judge every campaign on booked consults from your own records

</Checklist>
