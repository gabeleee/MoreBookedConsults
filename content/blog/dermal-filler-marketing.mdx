Dermal fillers carry more emotion than almost any med spa treatment, because patients have all seen filler go wrong.

That fear is the central fact of dermal filler marketing, and the practices that address it directly are the ones that book.

It shapes dermal filler SEO too: the patient typing "lip filler near me" into Google is already looking for someone they can trust with their face.

This guide is for med spa and injector practice owners, and it covers both halves of the job: getting found in search, and turning that search into a booked consult.

## <span className="he">🔎</span> Why SEO matters for a dermal filler clinic

Filler patients research before they book, and a lot of that research starts on Google.

Someone searching "dermal filler [your city]" has already chosen the treatment, so the only question left is who does it.

That makes search one of the few marketing channels where the patient arrives with intent already formed.

Organic rankings also sit outside the ad policies that limit injectable ads, and a page that ranks keeps bringing in searches long after the work is done.

### What SEO is, in plain terms

SEO (search engine optimization) is the work of making your website and your Google Business Profile the most relevant, trustworthy answer to the searches your patients type.

For a filler clinic it plays out in two places: the local map pack for "near me" searches, and the regular results below it, where treatment pages rank.

Google's own [help page on local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity (as of September 2026).

That page also names how many websites link to your business as one input, so backlinks count, alongside the topical authority you build by covering your treatments thoroughly.

## <span className="he">🏷️</span> Dermal filler keywords: treatment plus location

Patients rarely search "dermal filler" in the abstract.

They search a specific area and a specific place: "lip filler [city]," "cheek filler near me," "under-eye filler [neighborhood]."

Some search by product, so name the brands you actually use, like Juvéderm or Restylane, on the page.

Intent matters more than the exact words, because a ready-to-book search and a research search want different pages.

| Keyword type (example) | Intent | Page to target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treatment + city ("lip filler [city]") | Ready to book nearby | Your lip filler treatment page |
| Near me ("dermal filler near me") | A clinic close by | Google Business Profile, then your filler page |
| Treatment area ("under-eye filler [city]") | A specific result | A section or page for that area |
| Brand name ("Restylane [city]") | A product they've heard of | The filler page that names your products |
| Cost ("lip filler cost [city]") | Price before booking | The pricing section of the treatment page |
| Suburb ("cheek filler [nearby suburb]") | A closer option | A location page with real local content |
| Research ("how long does filler last") | Education | A blog post that links to the treatment page |

Start with the treatments you most want to sell and have the margin for, then cross each with the locations you serve.

Put the bottom-of-funnel searches first, the treatment-plus-city and near-me terms, because they map straight to a booking.

A national keyword tool may show little volume for a filler term in your city, which often understates real local demand.

## <span className="he">📄</span> Treatment pages that rank and book

Each filler treatment you want to rank for needs its own page on your website.

A single "injectables" page can't be the best answer to "lip filler [city]" and "jawline filler [city]" at the same time.

<Checklist>

- A title tag with the treatment and city, such as "Lip Filler in [City] | [Practice name]"
- A meta description that says what the treatment is, who performs it, and how to book
- One H1 naming the treatment and location, with H2 headings for the questions patients ask
- The injector's name, credentials, and experience with this treatment
- Before-and-after photos, used with written consent
- How you price it (per syringe or per area) and a starting point if you publish one
- Answers to the real questions: pain, downtime, how long it lasts, what happens if they don't like it
- A booking button that's visible without scrolling on a phone

</Checklist>

The [SEO checklist](/blog/medspa-seo-checklist/) covers the technical basics that let these pages rank at all.

### Experience, expertise, and credentials

Google's guidelines for its search quality raters hold health topics to a higher standard of trust, and filler pages fall squarely in that territory.

So show who actually injects: their name, role, credentials, training, and how long they've been doing filler work.

That same detail answers the patient's biggest question, whether this person has a good eye.

### Photos and image optimization

Before-and-after photos are your portfolio, and for fillers the portfolio is the pitch.

Use them only with written consent for each place they'll appear, and keep patient names out of file names, captions, and alt text.

Describe the treatment in the alt text instead ("lip filler before and after, one syringe"), and compress images so the page stays fast.

The [before-and-after photos guide](/blog/medspa-before-after-photos/) covers consent and presentation in detail.

### Schema markup on your own site

Schema markup is code that helps Google read what a page is about.

On your own site, that usually means LocalBusiness markup (or a more specific type such as MedicalBusiness) with your real name, address, and hours, matching your Google Business Profile.

Schema.org also has a MedicalProcedure type that can describe the treatment itself on a filler page.

Markup helps Google understand a page, but it doesn't replace good content.

## <span className="he">🗺️</span> Location pages and surrounding suburbs

If patients drive in from nearby suburbs, a location page can help you rank there.

It only works with real local content: directions and parking, who performs the treatment at that location, and answers to local questions.

Google's spam policies name pages targeted at specific cities or regions that funnel users to one page as doorway abuse, so don't clone one page twenty times with the city name swapped.

If you run more than one clinic, [multi-location SEO](/blog/medspa-multi-location-seo/) covers the structure.

## <span className="he">⭐</span> Reviews and NAP consistency

Google's help page says more reviews and positive ratings can help a business's local ranking.

For fillers, reviews do double duty, because a nervous patient reads them to decide whether you do natural work.

Ask once the result has settled, not on the day of treatment, and reply to every review without confirming the reviewer is a patient.

Any review or testimonial you feature must reflect a real patient's honest experience, and the [FTC testimonial rules](/blog/ftc-testimonial-rules-medspa/) spell out what that means.

The [reviews guide](/blog/medspa-get-more-reviews/) covers how to ask.

Your practice name, address, and phone number (NAP) also need to match everywhere they're listed.

Those listings are called citations, and old addresses or duplicate profiles make it harder for Google to trust which details are right.

The [citations and directories guide](/blog/medspa-citations-directories/) shows how to clean them up.

Run compliant ads, but expect to build trust through your portfolio, [reviews](/blog/medspa-get-more-reviews/), and referrals as much as through paid creative.

### Google Ads and cost per click

Google Ads is pay-per-click: you bid on searches like "lip filler [city]" and pay each time someone clicks, which is your cost per click (CPC).

Our view, from experience: Google Ads clicks for aesthetic treatments are very expensive, and Meta ads get med spas better results, so paid campaigns are where we run [managed Meta ads](/medspa-facebook-ads/) instead.
