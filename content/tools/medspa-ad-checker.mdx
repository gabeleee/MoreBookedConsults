## <span className="he">🏛️</span> Who actually enforces med spa ad rules

Most med spa owners think of ad rules as "whatever Meta rejects."

Meta is only the first gate, and the one that costs you the least when it says no.

<Cards>

<Card icon="target" title="Meta">

Rejects or limits ads that call out the reader's age, body or appearance, or that play on insecurity. A rejection costs you a day, but repeated ones can restrict the ad account.

</Card>

<Card icon="search" title="The FTC">

Requires health and results claims to be truthful and backed by evidence, and treats testimonials and before-and-afters as claims that the result is typical.

</Card>

<Card icon="shield" title="The FDA">

Polices claims about drugs and devices: what is FDA-approved, for which use, and how compounded drugs like semaglutide can be described.

</Card>

<Card icon="pin" title="State boards">

Medical and nursing boards set the rules on titles, credentials and advertising by licensed providers. They vary by state, and a complaint can start with one competitor's screenshot.

</Card>

</Cards>

## <span className="he">⚠️</span> The mistakes that show up most

Talking to the reader about their body is the most common reason med spa ads get rejected.

"Over 40?", "your wrinkles" and "tired of your double chin?" all imply something about the person reading, which Meta's personal attributes policy doesn't allow.

The fix is almost always the same: talk about the treatment and the result, not about the reader.

Promising outcomes is the next most common problem.

"Guaranteed results", "permanent" and "look 10 years younger" are promises no aesthetic treatment can keep for everyone, and the FTC treats an unprovable promise as deceptive.

Downplaying risk sits right behind it.

"Painless", "zero downtime" and "no side effects" are easy to write and hard to defend for an injectable, a laser or a prescription drug.

FDA wording trips up a lot of otherwise careful ads.

FDA approval belongs to a specific product for a specific use, so a "FDA-approved lip flip" is a false claim: the lip flip is an off-label use.

Weight loss ads carry the most risk right now.

Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide are not the brand-name drugs, and ads that call them "generic Ozempic" or "the same as Wegovy" are exactly what the FDA has been sending warning letters about.

## <span className="he">🧭</span> What a good score does and doesn't mean

A score of 90 or higher means the checker didn't find the common wording problems.

It doesn't mean the ad will be approved, because Meta also reviews the image, the landing page and the targeting.

A score under 60 means the ad is likely to be rejected or to draw a complaint as written.

Rewrite it before you put money behind it.

Lines marked "worth a look" are borderline.

Read them the way a skeptical reviewer would, and change them if you can't defend the claim.
